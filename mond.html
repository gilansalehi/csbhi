MOND and Relativistic MOND Cosmology

Below one acceleration scale, galactic motion follows the visible matter with startling regularity. MOND treats that regularity as a law of nature rather than the average outcome of invisible halos.

Codex (OpenAI) · September 9, 2026 · Draft profile · Corrections invited

The case for MOND

The anomaly knows the baryons

Galaxy rotation curves are usually presented as evidence for dark matter, but their regularity poses a second question. The inferred halo does not act independently of the visible galaxy. At every measured radius, the total acceleration follows the acceleration predicted from stars and gas; changes in the baryonic distribution reappear as features in the rotation curve. Mordehai Milgrom proposed in 1983 that the relation is fundamental.[1–4]

MOND introduces a single acceleration scale, \(a_0\approx1.2\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Newtonian dynamics holds far above it. Far below it, the observed acceleration becomes the geometric mean of the Newtonian acceleration and \(a_0\). Flat rotation curves and the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation then follow immediately:

\[ g\simeq\sqrt{g_Na_0}, \qquad V_f^4=G M_b a_0. \]

Those were not retrospective curve fits. The law predicted how low-surface-brightness galaxies should rotate before their detailed kinematics were known, and later data displayed the acceleration relation across thousands of points in galaxies spanning orders of magnitude in mass and surface density. The remaining question is whether MOND is the low-acceleration limit of a complete theory—or an empirical clue that both dark-matter models and modified-gravity models must explain.[5–7]

Central move
Replace Newtonian dynamics below a universal acceleration scale \(a_0\).
Strongest result
The deep-MOND limit fixes the baryonic Tully–Fisher slope, normalization, and surface-brightness independence.
Relativistic programme
Construct tensor–vector–scalar field equations that recover MOND in galaxies and fit lensing, the CMB, and structure growth.
Empirical status
Galactic regularities are substantial; clusters and cosmological completion remain unsettled.

The construction

From an acceleration rule to field equations

In spherical symmetry the phenomenological law is often written

\[ \mu\!\left(\frac{g}{a_0}\right)\mathbf g=\mathbf g_N, \qquad \mu(x)\rightarrow \begin{cases} 1, & x\gg1,\\ x, & x\ll1. \end{cases} \]

The interpolation near \(a_0\) is not fixed by the two limits. Nor can this algebraic expression describe every nonspherical system while preserving energy and momentum. Bekenstein and Milgrom therefore derived AQUAL from an action, replacing the linear Poisson equation with[2]

\[ \nabla\!\cdot\!\left[ \mu\!\left(\frac{|\nabla\Phi|}{a_0}\right)\nabla\Phi \right]=4\pi G\rho. \]

QUMOND provides a computationally simpler action-based formulation by solving ordinary Poisson equations with an effective source constructed from the Newtonian field.[3] Both theories are nonrelativistic. They calculate galaxy dynamics and the external-field effect, but cannot define cosmological expansion, relativistic lensing, or gravitational waves.

Relativistic completion

MOND is a limit shared by different theories

Bekenstein's TeVeS demonstrated that a covariant theory could reproduce the MOND limit while giving light and matter the lensing response missing from earlier scalar models. Its metric, vector, and scalar fields made cosmology possible and established that MOND need not remain an isolated formula.[8]

Relativistic completion is not unique. Different actions reproduce nearly the same quasistatic galaxy law while predicting different gravitational waves, lensing, stability, and cosmology. The 2021 Skordis–Złośnik theory is an important modern example: its linear cosmology can reproduce the CMB temperature spectrum and matter power spectrum normally attributed to cold dark matter, and its quadratic action avoids ghost instabilities in the regime studied.[9]

The distinction became observationally consequential after GW170817. The nearly coincident gravitational and electromagnetic signals severely constrained relativistic MOND theories in which the two signals acquire substantially different propagation or Shapiro delays. That result excludes important TeVeS-like constructions without excluding the nonrelativistic MOND law itself.[17]

That achievement changes rather than eliminates the bookkeeping. Particle dark matter is absent, but the new gravitational fields carry perturbations that perform part of cold dark matter's cosmological work. A final theory must show that the same fixed action accounts for galaxies, clusters, lensing, the CMB, nonlinear structure, and gravitational waves without choosing a different effective MOND law in each regime.

Explanatory gains

What MOND saw first

The Tully–Fisher relation becomes a theorem of the limit

For an isolated finite mass, scale invariance gives \(V_f^4=GM_ba_0\). The fourth-power slope and absence of a radius term are fixed.

Surface brightness predicts the discrepancy

Diffuse galaxies enter the MOND regime at smaller radii and therefore show larger apparent mass discrepancies even when their baryonic masses resemble brighter systems.

Rotation-curve structure follows visible structure

Gas concentrations, stellar disks, and their local features feed directly into the predicted acceleration instead of being combined with an independently fitted halo profile.

The external field becomes observable

A subsystem's internal motion can depend on the ambient acceleration even when tidal gradients are weak. This strong-equivalence-principle violation is a distinctive modified-gravity target.[10]

One scale organizes several galaxy laws

The onset of mass discrepancy, the RAR, the BTFR normalization, characteristic surface densities, and halo-like accelerations all collect around \(a_0\).

Open problems

Where the galactic law stops carrying the theory

1. Galaxy clusters retain missing mass

Standard MOND reduces the cluster discrepancy but does not generally remove it. Strong and weak lensing still indicate an additional central component, supplied in proposed solutions by unseen baryons, massive neutrinos, new fields, or a modified MOND law.[11] A 2026 study using an integrated galaxy-wide initial mass function found that baryons and stellar remnants could account for at least (88%) of the MOND dynamical mass in 46 nearby clusters. The result substantially narrows the deficit, although it depends on the adopted stellar-population model and does not yet close the cluster case.[16]

2. MOND does not specify its relativistic parent

AQUAL and QUMOND are complete nonrelativistic theories, but many covariant actions share their low-acceleration limit. Galactic success therefore does not select TeVeS, the Skordis–Złośnik theory, or another completion.[4, 8, 9]

3. Precision cosmology needs dark-matter-like dynamics

The CMB and early structure require gravitational potentials to grow before baryons decouple. Relativistic MOND models can provide that behavior through additional fields, but the required sector must then reproduce the rest of the cosmological evidence with fewer assumptions than particle dark matter.

4. Merging clusters test matter against curvature

Systems in which hot gas, galaxies, and lensing peaks separate cannot be judged from rotation curves. A relativistic MOND theory must evolve the collision and predict the lensing map using its actual fields and any residual unseen mass.

5. The value of \(a_0\) is unexplained

The approximate relation \(a_0\sim cH_0/(2\pi)\) has suggested a cosmological origin since MOND's beginning, but the nonrelativistic law inserts \(a_0\). No consensus derivation fixes its value or decides whether it is constant.[1, 12]

6. The interpolation region carries freedom

The Newtonian and deep-MOND limits are sharp; the function joining them is not. Solar-System constraints, the external-field effect, and detailed rotation curves test that freedom, but different formulations need not share one interpolating function.

Evidence and tests

The acceleration-scale test

Galactic regularity

2,693 radii · 153 galaxies · 0.11 dex scatter

The SPARC RAR relates observed and baryonic acceleration across varied morphologies, masses, sizes, and gas fractions. The reported scatter is largely observational; the fitted scale is \(g_\dagger\approx1.2\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).[5]

Prior prediction

\(M_b\propto V_f^4\), independent of galaxy size

Gas-rich galaxies isolate the baryonic mass from stellar mass-to-light uncertainties and return a BTFR consistent with the deep-MOND normalization, corresponding to \(a_0=(1.3\pm0.3)\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).[6]

Claimed distinctive signal

External-field effect detected above \(4\sigma\)

A blind SPARC analysis found rotation-curve behavior correlated with independently estimated galactic environments, as MOND's external-field effect predicts. The inference depends on environmental reconstruction and galaxy modelling and remains an active target for replication.[10]

New redshift test

\(a_0(z\!\sim\!1)=2.38^{+0.12}_{-0.10}\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)

MUSE-DARK III reports a higher characteristic acceleration and approximately 0.17 dex intrinsic scatter in 79 galaxies over \(0.33<z<1.44\). A strict universal constant is disfavored by the reported analysis; an evolving MOND scale and evolving galaxy–halo relation remain competing interpretations.[13, 14]

Competing explanation

ΛCDM can also generate an RAR

Galaxy–halo models in ΛCDM can produce a tight radial acceleration relation. The discriminating questions are no longer whether a relation exists, but whether its normalization, scatter, residual correlations, external-field behavior, and redshift evolution follow without tuning.[15]

Missing calculation

One action from recombination to galaxies

Choose a relativistic action and its parameters before fitting individual systems, then jointly predict the CMB, lensing, nonlinear structure, clusters, local RAR, external-field effect, and redshift evolution of \(a_0\).

The questions

What MOND answers—and leaves open

Gravity

AQUAL and QUMOND provide conserved nonrelativistic field theories, while covariant completions demonstrate that MOND can coexist with lensing and cosmology. No unique relativistic or quantum theory has been selected.
Singularities

The low-acceleration modification does not generically resolve the Big Bang or black-hole singularities. That question belongs to the chosen relativistic completion.
Expansion

Relativistic MOND theories can support viable backgrounds, but late acceleration is usually supplied by Λ or an additional field sector. The numerical link between \(a_0\) and the cosmic expansion scale remains suggestive rather than derived.
Beginning

Modern covariant models can reproduce linear CMB and matter spectra. They do not yet provide a uniquely selected primordial state, inflationary mechanism, or alternative beginning.
Matter

MOND removes the need for independently fitted dark halos in many galaxies. Clusters retain a mass deficit, and relativistic cosmology requires additional gravitational degrees of freedom that mimic part of dark matter's early role.
Constants

The acceleration \(a_0\) organizes galaxy phenomenology but is inserted by the effective law. Its value, possible redshift evolution, and relation to \(H\) or Λ remain open.

What comes next

Promote the galactic law into a cosmological theory

MOND has earned a place in fundamental cosmology through prediction, not merely dissatisfaction with dark matter. It anticipated the behavior of diffuse galaxies, fixes the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation in its deep limit, and organizes resolved galaxy dynamics around one acceleration scale. Any dark-matter theory must explain why that economical law works as well as it does.

The law is not yet the theory. Clusters require more gravity or more matter, and covariant completions add fields whose cosmological behavior is not fixed by Milgrom's formula. The Skordis–Złośnik construction shows that a relativistic MOND cosmology can reach the CMB and matter spectrum; the next standard is one action surviving the nonlinear and strong-lensing regimes without changing its rules.

Redshift evolution now makes the acceleration scale itself an experimental object. If \(a_0\) is constant, the intermediate-redshift result must yield to systematic correction. If it evolves, its functional relation to the cosmological background becomes part of the theory. Either outcome turns MOND's oldest numerical coincidence into one of its cleanest routes to a verdict.
