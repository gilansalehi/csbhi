Conformal Gravity

Conformal gravity asks whether local scale symmetry belongs in gravity as well as particle physics. The answer changes galactic dynamics, vacuum energy, and quantum gravity at once.

Codex (OpenAI) · September 8, 2026 · Draft profile · Corrections invited

The case for conformal gravity

Make scale symmetry fundamental

The Standard Model becomes more symmetric as masses disappear. Gravity moves the other way: Newton's constant fixes an absolute scale and ordinary quantization loses control. Conformal gravity asks whether local scale invariance should govern spacetime too. In four dimensions, that demand selects the Weyl-squared action and replaces a dimensionful gravitational coupling with a dimensionless one.[1–3]

The change reaches directly into galaxies. The fourth-order field equation contains the familiar Newtonian potential, a linear contribution from visible matter and the cosmic background, and a quadratic term from large-scale inhomogeneity. Rotation curves can then be fitted without surrounding every galaxy with an independently shaped dark halo. Local motion becomes a measurement of the universe beyond the galaxy.[4–7]

One symmetry therefore attacks three foundational problems at once: the quantum behavior of gravity, the missing-mass inference in galaxies, and the gravitational meaning of vacuum energy. If the programme survives precision lensing and cosmology, dark matter and the cosmological-constant problem may prove to be symptoms of using the wrong field equation.

Central move
Require local conformal invariance and use the Weyl-squared action instead of the Einstein–Hilbert action.

The construction

The Weyl tensor becomes the action

Einstein gravity begins with the Ricci scalar and Newton's constant. Conformal gravity begins with the part of curvature unchanged by local changes of scale:

\[ I_W=-\alpha_g\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g}\, C_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}C^{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}. \]

The dimensionless coupling \(\alpha_g\) and four derivatives improve the high-energy behavior of the propagator. Varying the action produces the Bach tensor rather than the Einstein tensor. The resulting field equation is fourth order, so its vacuum solution contains more radial structure than Schwarzschild alone.[2, 3]

For galactic motion, the extra freedom is the point. Luminous matter generates the familiar local \(1/R\) potential and a local linear potential. The surrounding cosmology supplies an additional universal linear term, while cosmic inhomogeneities supply a universal quadratic cutoff:

\[ v^2(R)=v_{\rm LOC}^2(R)+\frac{\gamma_0c^2R}{2}-\kappa c^2R^2. \]

The formula breaks the usual isolation of a galaxy. Local motion depends on local baryons and on the global universe. Mannheim and O'Brien applied that structure to 141 rotation curves with one adjustable quantity per galaxy—the luminous mass-to-light ratio—and no galactic dark halo.[4–6]

Explanatory gains

What conformal gravity adds to ΛCDM

Scale becomes a symmetry

Einstein gravity imports a dimensionful coupling. Conformal gravity starts scale-free and requires masses and effective gravitational scales to emerge through symmetry breaking.

Quantum gravity stays four-dimensional

The fourth-order propagator is power-counting renormalizable. Mannheim and collaborators argue that its non-Hermitian but PT-symmetric quantum formulation is unitary, offering a quantum-gravity route without new dimensions or fundamental strings.[3, 7]

Galaxies stop being isolated systems

The universal linear and quadratic terms connect galactic dynamics directly to cosmology. What ΛCDM assigns to a separate halo can instead arise from the response of one gravitational field to local and global matter.

Rotation curves need no halo profile

The published 141-galaxy analysis spans high- and low-surface-brightness spirals, dwarfs, and tidal dwarfs. Its universal coefficients remain fixed across the sample; the fitted mass-to-light ratio belongs to the visible galaxy.[4]

The RAR and Tully–Fisher relation gain a field-theory route

The same potential reproduces the observed coupling between baryonic and total acceleration and yields a Tully–Fisher-like relation without treating the MOND interpolation function as fundamental.[5, 6]

Vacuum energy can be large without gravitating normally

Mannheim's cosmology attacks the cosmological-constant problem by changing the effective cosmological response rather than forcing the vacuum energy itself to be tiny. Cosmic acceleration becomes part of that response.[8, 9]

Empirical contact

The case begins with galaxies

Conformal gravity has gone beyond qualitative modified-gravity arguments. It provides an explicit relativistic action, derives a galactic potential, fixes universal coefficients across a large sample, and confronts resolved rotation curves. Stellar mass-to-light ratios remain fitted, and the result is not yet a joint cosmological fit, but the theory's distinctive gravitational law has reached observable data.

The radial acceleration relation provides a second representation of the same success. Conformal gravity reproduces the baryon–acceleration correlation from its potential rather than imposing the usual square-root MOND law. A recent Mannheim analysis further identifies a cosmologically fixed low-acceleration floor. Its evolution with redshift remains an especially clean opportunity for the theory to make a new prediction.[5, 10]

Open problems

What remains unresolved

1. Precision cosmology has not been reproduced

A dark-matter alternative must recover the CMB acoustic peaks, baryon acoustic oscillations, primordial nucleosynthesis, lensing, cluster dynamics, and the matter power spectrum with one consistent parameter set. Conformal perturbation equations exist, but no conformal-gravity equivalent of the ΛCDM joint likelihood has yet cleared that standard.[11]

2. The massive-particle sector affects the galaxy calculation

Local conformal symmetry forbids fixed particle masses; a scalar sector must generate them. Hobson and Lasenby argue that treating this scalar consistently changes the physical geodesics and removes the flat region attributed to the Mannheim–Kazanas metric. The rotation-curve mechanism therefore carries a live dispute about conformal frame, matter coupling, and which metric massive bodies actually follow.[12]

3. Lensing and clusters remain independent tests

Rotation curves probe nonrelativistic motion. Light deflection probes the metric differently. A 2023 analysis of Abell 370 and Abell 2390 found that the Mannheim–Kazanas lensing prediction could not explain those clusters without dark matter. A successful completion must fit motion and lensing with the same fields and boundary conditions.[13]

4. Quantum unitarity remains contested

Mannheim's PT-symmetric quantization removes the negative-norm ghost found by the usual Hermitian treatment.[7] An independent BRST analysis instead finds an indefinite spin-2 sector and nonunitary scattering.[14] Renormalizability is a major gain; quantum consistency still depends on which construction survives a shared calculation.

5. Fourth-order dynamics need a complete physical prescription

Extra derivatives bring extra solutions, boundary data, and modes. The theory must specify which states are physical, how stable initial-value evolution works through nonlinear and strong-field regimes, and why unwanted branches do not appear in nature.[14, 16]

6. The universal galactic terms need an epoch law

The fitted coefficients have cosmological dimensions and are interpreted as global contributions, but their redshift dependence has not been derived into a direct observational curve. High-redshift rotation curves and the evolving RAR can test whether the global terms are truly cosmological or merely successful present-day constants.[4, 10]

Evidence and tests

Can the galactic law survive lensing?

Best quantitative result

141 rotation curves · three universal coefficients

The analysis used \(\gamma^*=5.42\times10^{-41}\,{\rm cm}^{-1}\), \(\gamma_0=3.06\times10^{-30}\,{\rm cm}^{-1}\), and \(\kappa=9.54\times10^{-54}\,{\rm cm}^{-2}\), fitting one luminous mass-to-light ratio per galaxy and no galactic halo.[4]

Strongest adverse result

0 of 2 rich-cluster lensing tests passed

An independent analysis found that the Mannheim–Kazanas prediction could not explain Abell 370 or Abell 2390 without dark matter.[13] The sample is small, and the status of the complete matter/scalar sector remains disputed.

Decisive test

One field configuration must bend light and move matter

Fix the theory from baryons and cosmological boundary data, then predict both resolved dynamics and relativistic lensing before either dataset is fitted. Passing the same test across galaxies and clusters would distinguish a gravitational law from a rotation-curve prescription.

Missing calculation

Produce the joint cosmological likelihood

No published conformal-gravity calculation yet matches the CMB acoustic spectrum, BAO, supernovae, lensing, and structure growth with one parameter set. Absence of that calculation limits reach; it is not itself a failed prediction.

The questions

What conformal gravity answers—and leaves open

Gravity

A renormalizable four-dimensional field theory exists; its quantum unitarity and complete matter coupling remain contested.[7, 12, 14]
Singularities

Conformal gravity permits new strong-field solutions but has not established a generic replacement for collapse or Big-Bang singularities.[2, 3]
Expansion

The framework gives cosmic acceleration and a proposed vacuum-energy mechanism; a precision expansion and perturbation fit remains outstanding.[8, 9, 11]
Beginning

No complete early-universe mechanism derives the observed initial state and primordial spectrum.[11]
Matter

Galactic dark matter is replaced by geometry, while the full cluster, CMB, lensing, and structure evidence remains unresolved.[4, 11, 13]
Constants

Local scale symmetry constrains the form of the action and reframes mass generation, but observed scales and universal galactic coefficients are not yet derived from first principles.[1, 4, 12]

What comes next

From galaxy fits to cosmology

One symmetry changes the gravitational action, improves its ultraviolet behavior, removes fundamental mass scales, alters the cosmological response to vacuum energy, and places the universe inside the potential of every galaxy. The rotation-curve program shows that the idea can calculate rather than merely redescribe.

The next step is a single relativistic cosmological solution—matter sector included—that fits supernovae, BAO, the CMB, lensing, and structure growth while retaining the successful galactic potential. The solution should also predict how \(\gamma_0\), \(\kappa\), and the low-acceleration floor evolve with redshift.

ΛCDM already connects those observations in one precision framework. Reproducing them without dark matter would extend conformal gravity's serious result in galaxies into a new cosmology—and directly test its claim that global cosmology governs local galactic dynamics.
