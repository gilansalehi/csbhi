f(R) Gravity

Einstein made the gravitational action linear in curvature. Let curvature respond nonlinearly and geometry itself can drive inflation, alter structure growth, and imitate dark energy.

Codex (OpenAI) · September 9, 2026 · Draft profile · Corrections invited

The case for f(R) gravity

The simplest gravitational action need not be the final one

General relativity builds its action from the Ricci scalar \(R\), the simplest scalar measure of spacetime curvature. Nothing in covariance requires that dependence to remain linear at every scale. Quantum field theory in curved spacetime generates higher-curvature terms, and the first correction one can write is \(R^2\). In 1980, Alexei Starobinsky showed that such a term drives an early phase of accelerated expansion and exits naturally into the hot universe.[1, 2]

That economical change produced one of inflation's most successful quantitative models. For roughly fifty to sixty e-folds, \(R+R^2\) inflation predicts a scalar tilt near \(0.96\)–\(0.967\) and a tensor fraction of only a few parts in a thousand, directly in the region favored by CMB observations. The accelerated phase comes from gravity itself rather than a separately postulated inflaton potential.[2, 8, 9]

Replace \(R\) by a more general function \(f(R)\) and the same idea reaches late-time cosmology. Curvature acquires one additional scalar mode whose effective energy can accelerate expansion while a chameleon mechanism suppresses the fifth force in dense environments. A viable function could therefore connect inflation, cosmic acceleration, and measurable departures from GR without introducing an independent dark energy fluid.[3–7]

Central move
Replace the Einstein–Hilbert term \(R\) with a nonlinear function \(f(R)\).
Extra degree of freedom
Metric \(f(R)\) gravity is dynamically equivalent to a scalar–tensor theory with one scalar mode.
Strongest cosmological result
The \(R+R^2\) model gives a parameter-economical inflationary spectrum consistent with current CMB limits.
Empirical status
No departure from GR has been established; viable late-time models are increasingly constrained toward the GR limit.

The construction

Curvature becomes dynamical in a new way

This profile concerns metric \(f(R)\) gravity, in which the metric is the fundamental gravitational variable. Palatini and metric-affine theories use a different variational principle and have different dynamics. The metric action is

\[ S=\frac{c^3}{16\pi G}\int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\,f(R)+S_m[g_{\mu\nu},\psi]. \]

Writing \(F\equiv df/dR\), variation with respect to the metric gives[3, 4]

\[ F R_{\mu\nu}-\frac12 f g_{\mu\nu} -\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu F+g_{\mu\nu}\Box F =\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}. \]

The trace equation,

\[ 3\Box F+FR-2f=\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}T, \]

shows that \(F\) propagates. The theory can be rewritten as a scalar–tensor model with scalar \(\phi=F\) and potential \(V(\phi)=R(\phi)\phi-f[R(\phi)]\). What looked like higher-derivative gravity is therefore GR plus one specially coupled scalar degree of freedom. Stability and a healthy high-curvature limit require, over the relevant regime, conditions including \(F>0\) and \(f_{RR}>0\).[3–5]

Two curvature regimes

Inflationary success and late-time gravity are separate tests

Starobinsky inflation uses

\[ f(R)=R+\frac{R^2}{6M^2}. \]

At large curvature, the \(R^2\) term drives quasi-de Sitter expansion. In the scalar representation, the extra mode—the scalaron—rolls down a fixed plateau potential and later oscillates, reheating the universe. For \(N\) e-folds before inflation ends, the leading predictions are

\[ n_s\simeq1-\frac{2}{N}, \qquad r\simeq\frac{12}{N^2}. \]

Late-time models perform a different job at extremely low curvature. Hu–Sawicki and related functions are designed to approximate GR at high density, generate an accelerating background at cosmic density, and alter the growth of structure through a finite-range scalar force.[6]

\[ f(R)=R-m^2\frac{c_1(R/m^2)^n}{c_2(R/m^2)^n+1}. \]

The chameleon effect makes the scalar heavy in deep gravitational potentials and light on cosmological scales. Solar-System agreement and cosmological deviations can then coexist, but only within a narrowing parameter region.[6, 7]

Explanatory gains

What nonlinear curvature can accomplish

Inflation becomes gravitational dynamics

The scalaron and its potential are fixed by the \(R^2\) term. A separate elementary inflaton need not be introduced.

The primordial spectrum is sharply constrained

Once the number of e-folds is specified, the leading scalar tilt and tensor fraction occupy a narrow region rather than an arbitrary potential landscape.

Acceleration can move into geometry

Late expansion can arise from the nonlinear curvature response instead of an independently conserved dark-energy component.

Background and growth can separate

Two models can share nearly the same expansion history while producing different lensing, clustering, and halo abundances. The theory therefore makes tests beyond the supernova Hubble diagram.

GR is a controlled limit

When \(f(R)\rightarrow R-2\Lambda\) and the scalar becomes heavy, Einstein gravity is recovered over its tested domain.

Open problems

Where the freedom in \(f\) becomes a liability

1. A function is not a prediction

Covariance permits infinitely many choices of \(f(R)\). Stability, local tests, and cosmology eliminate large families, but no accepted principle selects the surviving function or relates its high- and low-curvature scales.[3, 4]

2. Screening carries astrophysical dependence

The same scalar force that changes structure growth must disappear in the Solar System and screened galaxies. Predictions then depend on environmental potentials, nonlinear halo physics, and baryonic modelling.[6, 7, 12]

3. Late-time viability approaches ΛCDM

Current structure and cluster measurements find no significant departure from GR. The parameter space with the largest observable effects is being excluded, leaving models whose expansion and growth increasingly resemble ΛCDM.[10–12]

4. Dark matter usually remains

Cosmological \(f(R)\) models generally retain cold dark matter to reproduce the CMB, lensing, clusters, and structure. They offer an alternative to dark energy more readily than an alternative to the entire dark sector.

5. One scalar does not quantize gravity

Higher-curvature terms are expected in an effective action, but an arbitrarily chosen \(f(R)\) does not provide a UV-complete quantum theory or determine the full tower of curvature corrections.

6. The two accelerations are not yet one theory

The coefficient required for primordial \(R^2\) inflation and the scale required for late acceleration are enormously different. A single technically natural function connecting both regimes has not been selected by established microphysics.[3, 4]

Evidence and tests

Inflation succeeds; late-time deviations remain absent

Successful prediction

\(n_s\simeq0.964\), \(r\simeq0.004\) for \(N=55\)

The leading Starobinsky predictions lie inside current CMB constraints, including Planck's \(n_s=0.9649\pm0.0042\) and the BICEP/Keck upper bound \(r_{0.05}<0.036\). The scalar amplitude fixes the mass scale \(M\); the tilt and small tensor signal then follow.[8, 9]

Current late-time result

\(\log_{10}|f_{R0}|<-4.12\) at 95%

The first eROSITA all-sky cluster sample, with weak-lensing calibration and varying neutrino mass, found no deviation from GR in the Hu–Sawicki model. The bound is model- and dataset-specific rather than universal.[10]

Model-independent growth test

\(\mu_0=0.05\pm0.22\), \(\Sigma_0=0.008\pm0.045\)

DESI full-shape clustering combined with CMB, DES weak lensing, and supernovae found the effective gravitational strength and lensing response consistent with GR. These parameters cover more theories than \(f(R)\) and therefore do not translate into one unique \(f_{R0}\) bound.[11]

Near-term test

Cross-check growth, lensing, and screening

Euclid, Rubin, CMB-S4, and deeper cluster surveys can compare the motion of matter with the lensing potential across redshift and environment. A viable model must fit those correlated signals with the same \(f_{R0}\), neutrino sector, and screening prescription.[13]

Missing calculation

Derive the function

The programme needs a microscopic principle that selects \(f(R)\), fixes its curvature scales, and controls the other higher-curvature operators expected at the same order.

The questions

What \(f(R)\) gravity answers—and leaves open

Gravity

The classical gravitational response gains one scalar degree of freedom and a testable scale dependence. The construction is an effective modification, not a completed quantum gravity.
Singularities

The \(R^2\) term controls a finite-curvature inflationary regime, but successful inflation does not by itself prove geodesic completeness. Generic black-hole and cosmological singularities are not removed by every viable \(f(R)\).
Expansion

High-curvature nonlinearities give a successful inflationary model; low-curvature nonlinearities can drive late acceleration. No accepted principle unifies the two scales or selects the late-time function.
Beginning

Starobinsky inflation explains flatness and generates primordial fluctuations, while its own initial state and the pre-inflationary boundary remain outside the model.
Matter

The theory changes gravity rather than the particle inventory. Standard baryons, radiation, neutrinos, and usually cold dark matter are imported.[3, 4]
Constants

The inflationary mass \(M\), late-time curvature scale, and functional parameters are fitted or chosen. Their hierarchy is not derived from the classical action.

What comes next

Preserve the inflationary triumph and expose the late-time scalar

The fairest verdict divides the programme in two. \(R+R^2\) inflation is a landmark: a simple curvature correction made quantitative predictions decades before precision CMB cosmology and remains one of the best-fitting inflationary models. That success establishes the physical power of nonlinear curvature.

Late-time \(f(R)\) gravity faces a different record. Viable functions can reproduce the expansion history and evade local tests, but observations have not found their additional scalar force. As screening and structure constraints tighten, the remaining models approach GR plus Λ in the regimes we can measure.

A breakthrough would connect the two regimes instead of choosing them independently: one derived effective action, one stable cosmic history, and one scalar whose predicted effects appear consistently in inflation, lensing, clustering, and screened systems. Without that connection, \(f(R)\) remains a productive language containing an exceptional inflationary model and many increasingly constrained theories of late acceleration.
